I did my undergraduate degree in Civil Engineering at the Indian Institute of Technology Bombay (2012–2016), where Prof. Tom Mathew and faculty mentor Prof. Raaj Ramsankaran first got me hooked on the problem of how people actually move through cities, rather than just how roads are built for them.

That curiosity took me to the University of Texas at Austin in 2016 for an MSc in Transportation, working under Prof. Chandra R. Bhat — one of the field's great teachers of choice modelling. My thesis quantified what drives household vehicle travel, a question I'm still, in one form or another, asking a decade later.

From Austin I moved to London in 2019 for a PhD in Transportation Engineering at Imperial College, supervised by Prof. Aruna Sivakumar and examined by Prof. Dan Graham and Prof. Jonas De Vos. My dissertation tackled the endogeneity and consideration-set problems that quietly undermine most residential location choice models — unglamorous, technical work, but the kind that changes how confidently you can say anything about why people live where they live.

After finishing in 2023, I took up a lectureship and research post at the University of Limerick, working on the SEAI-funded ISCycle project — my first real foray into transport-energy behaviour in an Irish context.

In 2024 I joined Trinity College Dublin as a Research Fellow, and it's been the busiest and most rewarding chapter yet. I'm Co-Principal Investigator on CIRCUIT, a project on causal inference for resilient, integrated urban transport funded by the Sustainable Energy Authority of Ireland, and Co-Investigator on INTEGRID-RISE, funded by the EPA, looking at infrastructure resilience. I've also contributed to the UCD-led CONUNDRUM project, and I'm part of the team developing a major ERC Synergy Grant proposal with colleagues at Delft University of Technology and the University of Thessaly.

Alongside the research, I've taught transportation modelling, statistics, and infrastructure systems at Trinity, Limerick, Imperial, and UT Austin — and I care a lot about that side of the job: student-centred, inclusive teaching, and being genuinely useful to the postgraduates I supervise, not just a name on their committee.

In 2027 I'll be moving on again, to Loughborough University as a Lecturer in Transport and Energy Systems — a role that sits right at the intersection of everything I've been building toward: transportation engineering, infrastructure systems, energy systems, and applied econometrics, all in service of designing infrastructure that's not just functional but genuinely sustainable and resilient.

What ties all of it together, from Mumbai to Austin to London to Dublin and soon Loughborough, is the same underlying question: how do people make the travel, housing, and energy decisions that quietly shape whether a city ends up sustainable, equitable, or resilient — and how do we build the statistical tools honest enough to actually answer that.
